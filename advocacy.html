Track your period.

A tracker turns "I think it's been worse lately" into a pattern a doctor can actually read. These apps are all built for younger users. Age ranges are the ones each app suggests for itself.

Ages 10–17

Luna Period Tracker

A straightforward period and symptom tracker aimed at younger teens. Log start and end dates, flow, cramps, and mood, and watch the pattern build over a few cycles.

Ages 8–16

Oky

A period tracker created with and for girls by UNICEF. Plain-language explanations sit alongside the calendar, which makes it a good first tracker if you've only had a few periods.

Ages 9–17

Ove

A teen-focused period app that pairs cycle tracking with short explainers about what's happening in your body that week, rather than just marking days on a calendar.

Ages 12–19+

Aavia

Tracks symptoms across your whole cycle and connects them to hormone patterns. Useful if you're trying to show a provider that your symptoms cluster at the same point every month.

Ages 8–19+

Teena

A guide-first app: period tracking plus answers to the questions people are often too embarrassed to ask out loud. Good if you want the explanations more than the calendar.

ⓘ

Before you download

Uncover isn't affiliated with any of these apps and gets nothing for listing them. Search the App Store or Google Play by name, read what the app does with your data, and remember that anything stored on your phone can be seen by whoever has your phone. A paper calendar counts as a tracker too.

03

Questions to ask your doctor.

Say them out loud once before you go in. They're much easier to ask the second time.

"What could be causing this, and how do we find out?"

"Is this something I should track and follow up on, or address now?"

"What are all my treatment options, not just the first one?"

"Can you refer me to a specialist if this doesn't improve?"

"What symptoms would mean I should come back sooner?"

"Can we write down a plan so I remember what we discussed?"

05

Medical terminology.

Words that come up a lot in reproductive health appointments.

Laparoscopy+
A minor surgery using a small camera to look inside the pelvis, currently the only way to confirm an endometriosis diagnosis with certainty.
Transvaginal ultrasound+
An imaging test using a wand-shaped scanner to get a clearer picture of the uterus and ovaries than an external ultrasound.
GnRH agonist+
A type of medication that temporarily pauses your cycle and lowers estrogen, sometimes used to manage endometriosis or adenomyosis pain.
Menorrhagia+
The medical term for abnormally heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding.
Referral+
When your regular doctor sends you to see a specialist, like a gynecologist, for something outside their scope.
Hormonal IUD+
A small device placed in the uterus that releases hormones to reduce bleeding and pain, one option for managing several reproductive conditions, not just contraception.
06

Chronic pain affects more than your body.

Living with unexplained pain, or waiting years for a diagnosis, takes a toll. You may experience anxiety, depression, stress, isolation, or fatigue, that's real, and support for it matters too.

🧠

You're allowed to be frustrated

Feeling dismissed by doctors, teachers, or even friends is exhausting. That frustration is a reasonable response, not an overreaction.

💛

Coping isn't fixing

Heat, rest, movement, and talking to someone you trust won't cure a condition, but they can make hard days more bearable while you get answers.

🗣

Find your person

A school counselor, nurse, trusted teacher, or older sibling, pick one person to loop in so you're not carrying this alone.

Healthy coping strategies

  • Talking with someone you trust
  • Joining a support group
  • Gentle exercise
  • Journaling
  • Mindfulness
  • Counselling
  • Getting enough sleep
